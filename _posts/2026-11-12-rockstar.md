If games were really about fantasy fulfillment, we would see a lot more games that let you play a rockstar. It is possible that the moment has already passed. Rap has long supplanted rock and influencers are now living the lifestyles of the rich and famous. If Charli could not bring herself to care about rock on a song literally called "Rock Music", how much can we?

So, for our first game, we must look at *Rockstar! (1989)*, a spreadsheet simulator from the late '80s. Like many games and game designers from the '80s, it was surprisingly preoccupied with drugs, which end up being the greatest threat that you face in the game.

It starts with a fairly simple system of needing inspiration to make music and needing drugs for inspiration and a fairly large menu of drugs to choose from, but this proves easy to game and it is fairly trivial to rise up the charts without getting addicted.

So, once you cross a threshold of fame, the game just makes you addicted to drugs itself. No matter what choices the player makes, the character will develop addictions over and over again.

This lack of autonomy is key to the fantasy of being a rockstar. To have power is to have the burden of needing to make the right choice. We imagine the rockstar as unable to choose sobriety, that opportunity and society together place the rockstar in check, leaving drugs as the only possible move.

Of course, this is reinforced by the profitability of this pose. You do not need rock and roll to have sex and drugs, you need rock and roll to make money from sex and drugs. *Rockstar!* simply literalizes this.

Here is the fantasy of the rockstar in tech; a person who transgresses against the boundaries of society and common sense in order to make ungodly amounts of money. This rockstar does not need a union or a stable career or a healthy workplace. They just need a space where someone can profit off their self-destruction.

*Family (2020)* speaks instead of the reality of the rockstar. In it, you trawl through old clippings to figure out which musicians played in which times for which bands. It is warm and heartfelt and indeed familial.


I needed this theming. Deduction games often feel like puzzles for the sake of puzzles to me. With *Family*, it felt like my effort was an homage to the passion of a bunch of musicians in a place and time, even if they are fictional.

I hope that the musicians in the game ended up doing well. I hope that they grew past these forgotten bands and found happiness, however it came. I hope the same for me and I hope the same for you.
